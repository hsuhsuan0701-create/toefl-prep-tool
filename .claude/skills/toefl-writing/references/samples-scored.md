# Scored Writing illustrations (severity anchors)

These are **this project's own illustrations, not ETS's samples**: invented prompts, invented
responses, and scores this project assigns against its own rubrics (`rubric-email.md`,
`rubric-academic-discussion.md`). They exist to show where the band lines fall, especially the
lines this task most often gets wrong. ETS publishes its own scored samples in the *Updated TOEFL
iBT® Test Overview* (2026), pp. 14–17; a user who installs those as `references/official/
samples-scored.md` (see `docs/official-rubrics.md`) gets them in place of this file.

## What ETS's own samples establish

ETS's overview scores one response per Writing task: an email at **4** and an academic discussion
post at **5**. There is no published Writing sample below 4, so everything said here about bands 3
and lower is reasoned from the rubric descriptions, not from an ETS exemplar.

The two published samples still settle the question these illustrations are built around. The
discussion post that earns a 5 is not clean: it has a subject–verb agreement slip, a possessive
and number error, a dropped article and a stray hyphen. The email that earns a 4 has a similar
number of surface errors, most of them misspellings, and ETS's comment pins the lost band on one
clause whose verb phrase falls apart, so that the reader cannot tell what the writer meant without
rebuilding the sentence.

The rule that follows, and that the illustrations below apply:

> **Count consequence, not volume.** Slips a reader fixes without slowing down — a misspelling, a
> missing article, an agreement error — are compatible with a 5. An error that makes the reader
> stop and reconstruct the meaning costs a band, and one is enough to move a 5 to a 4.

## Write an Email — the damaged camera

**Prompt.** You borrowed a video camera from your university's media center for a class project.
When you opened the case at home, the lens cap was missing and the camera's screen had a long
scratch that you did not cause. The camera is due back this Friday.

Write an email to the media center staff. Your email should:

- Explain what you found when you opened the case.
- Ask what you should do so that you are not charged for the damage.
- Suggest a way the center could prevent this problem for other students.

To: mediacenter@northfield.edu · Subject: Camera loan returned with damage

### E-1 — Score 5

> Dear Media Center staff,
>
> I borrowed camera kit 14 on Monday for my documentary class, and when I opened the case at home I
> found two problems. The lens cap was missing, and there is a long scratch across the screen. I
> definately did not cause either of them; I photographed the screen right away and attached the
> pictures, so you can see the condition it was in when it reached me.
>
> Since the camera is due on Friday, could you tell me how to return it without being charged for
> damage I didn't do? I am happy to bring it in early so someone can inspect it with me at the desk.
>
> For the future, it might help if staff checked each kit with the student at checkout and noted
> any damage on the loan form. That would protect both the center and the students who borrow
> equipment after a careless user.
>
> Thank you for you help,
> Priya Raman

**Why 5.** All three instructions are carried out and developed with the specifics a staff member
needs (kit number, timing, the photos, an offer to come in), which is the band-5 line for
**communicative goal & social conventions** and **elaboration & cohesion**. The register is polite
without grovelling, and the request is framed as a question. Sentence structures vary naturally.
The two slips — *definately*, *you help* — are the kind a reader corrects without slowing down,
which band 5 allows under **mechanics** and **grammar & vocabulary**.

### E-2 — Score 4 (one broken clause)

> Dear Media Center staff,
>
> I borrowed camera kit 14 on Monday for my documentary class. When I opened the case at home, the
> lens cap was missing and the screen has a long scratch. I took photos right away and attached
> them to this email.
>
> I want to ask how I can return the camera without paying for this damage. The scratch, which when
> I opened it at home already there, so I hope it is not counted to me. I can bring it on Thursday
> if it is better.
>
> Maybe staff could check every camera together with the student before the loan starts, so problems
> like this are noticed earlier.
>
> Best regards,
> Priya Raman

**Why 4 and not 5.** The email is complete, organised and appropriately polite — on
**communicative goal & social conventions** and **elaboration & cohesion** it is close to E-1. What
costs the band is one sentence: *"The scratch, which when I opened it at home already there, so I
hope it is not counted to me."* The relative clause has no verb, the main clause never arrives, and
the reader has to rebuild the intended meaning. That single error of consequence, plus the less
exact *counted to me*, puts **grammar & vocabulary** at "suitable but less exact, a small number of
errors". Compare E-1, which has more surface slips and keeps its 5.

### E-3 — Score 4 (adequate rather than precise)

> Hello,
>
> I borrowed a camera from you for a class project. When I opened it, the lens cap was not there and
> there is a scratch on the screen. It was not me who did it.
>
> Please tell me what I need to do so I don't have to pay for this. The camera has to be returned
> on Friday.
>
> I think you should check the cameras more carefully before giving them to students. Then this
> will not happen again.
>
> Thanks,
> Priya

**Why 4.** Nearly error-free, and every instruction is done — but each is done at the minimum. The
development is sufficient rather than strong (no kit number, no evidence, no offer to help), the
word choice is suitable rather than exact, and *"It was not me who did it"* and *"you should check
the cameras more carefully"* are slightly blunt for a request to staff. That is the band-4 profile
on three dimensions at once, even with clean **mechanics**.

### E-4 — Score 3

> Hi,
>
> I am write about the camera I borrow last week. The lens cap is lost and the screen have scratch,
> but this is not my fault because it was already like this.
>
> I don't want pay money for it. What I should do?
>
> You need to check the cameras before student take it.
>
> Priya

**Why 3.** The core purpose comes through: the problem, the worry about being charged and a
suggestion are all there, so the task is broadly accomplished. But development is partial (each
point is a single sentence), the tone is noticeably off for a request (*"I don't want pay money"*,
*"You need to check"*), and errors in verb forms and word order (*I am write*, *I borrow*, *What I
should do?*) are noticeable throughout. Meaning survives, which keeps it above 2.

### E-5 — Score 2

> Hello. Camera is broken when I take. Screen scratch and cap no. I return Friday. Not pay because
> not my. Thank you.

**Why 2.** The writer is plainly trying to answer, and a reader can guess at the situation, but the
email mostly fails at its job: the third instruction is missing, there is almost no development,
and the language is narrow and full of errors. It sits at 2 rather than 1 because some connected
sentence-level language survives (*"I return Friday"*, *"Thank you"*) and none of it is copied from
the prompt.

## Write for an Academic Discussion — personal finance courses

**Prompt.** In an education policy course, the professor has opened a question on the class
discussion board. Add a post that gives and supports your own opinion, in your own words.

**Dr. Okafor:** Many universities let students graduate without ever studying how to budget, borrow
or invest. Some educators argue that a short course in personal finance should be required of every
student, whatever their major. Should universities make such a course mandatory? Why or why not?

**Mei:** Yes. Most of us sign our first loan or lease while we are still students, and nobody
explains interest rates or credit scores to us. A required course would prevent mistakes that
follow people for years.

**Daniel:** I disagree. Degree programs are already full, and anything a student needs to know about
budgeting is free online. Making the course compulsory would push out an elective that students
actually chose.

### D-1 — Score 5

> Daniel is right that the free material exists, but in my experience almost nobody looks for it
> until they are already in trouble, and that is exactly why the course should be required. When I
> signed my first apartment lease, I did not understand that the deposit was not refundable, and I
> lost three hundred dollars that a single class session would of saved me. A required course
> reaches the students who would never go searching for a budgeting video. It also doesn't have to
> crowd out electives: a one-credit online module that students finish during there first year
> would cover loans, credit, and basic saving without changing any degree plan. The cost of the
> course is small compared to the cost of the mistakes it prevents.

**Why 5.** A clear position that answers Dr. Okafor and engages Daniel's strongest point directly,
which is the band-5 line for **argument & engagement**. The support is relevant and worked out: a
personal example with a concrete consequence, then a practical answer to the scheduling objection
(**support & cohesion**). Structures vary and the wording is precise (*reaches the students who
would never go searching*). The two errors — *would of* and *there* for *their* — are slips a
reader repairs automatically, which band 5 allows.

### D-2 — Score 4

> I agree with Mei that the course should be mandatory. Many students make financial mistakes
> because they do not have knowledge about loans and credit cards. For example, my cousin used his
> credit card for everything in his first year and he had a big debt after. If he took a finance
> course, he could avoid this problem. Daniel says that information is online, but students usually
> do not search it. I think one course is not too much time and it is very useful for the life
> after university.

**Why 4.** Relevant, easy to follow, and supported with an example and a reply to Daniel, so
**argument & engagement** and **support & cohesion** both reach "relevant and developed enough".
It falls short of 5 on precision rather than error count: the example stays general (*a big debt
after*), the wording is suitable but plain (*very useful for the life after university*), and there
are a few errors (*If he took … he could avoid*, *search it*). None of them makes the reader stop.

### D-3 — Score 3

> In my opinion the course is good idea. Because money is important for everyone and students
> doesn't know about it. Also the interest is very dangerous. Daniel say it is online but online
> have many wrong information so is better to learn in class. So I think universities should make
> it mandatory for all students.

**Why 3.** Mostly relevant and mostly understandable: a position is taken and Daniel is answered.
But a piece of the support is missing — *"the interest is very dangerous"* is asserted and never
explained — which is the band-3 line for **support & cohesion**. Errors in agreement, articles and
sentence boundaries (*is good idea*, *students doesn't*, *online have many wrong information*) are
noticeable throughout, though each sentence can still be understood.

### D-4 — Score 2

> I think finance course is good and also not good. Is good because student need money. But
> Daniel say is full. University have many class and student is busy so maybe not. I like the
> class about money.

**Why 2.** The writer is trying to take part, but the ideas are only partly relevant and barely
developed: the post never settles on an answer to the professor's question, and *"student need
money"* does not support either side. The range of structures is narrow and the errors build up
until the ideas are hard to follow. It stays above 1 because there are ideas of the writer's own,
however unclear.

## How to use these anchors

- **Over-penalising surface errors** is the first failure mode. E-1 and D-1 each carry slips and
  keep their 5. Do not tally typos into a cascade of deductions.
- **Under-penalising a broken clause** is the second. E-2 is organised, polite and on task, and
  still drops to 4 for one sentence whose meaning has to be rebuilt.
- **Above 4, separate 5 from 4 on precision and development**, as E-3 and D-2 show, not on how many
  errors there are.
- **Task completion is its own question.** An email that leaves out one of its three instructions
  (E-5) has not done its job, however clean the prose; that is a `completeness` deduction. So is a
  discussion post that only repeats a classmate's view and gives no reasoning of the writer's
  own, and a post that stops well short of the 100 words the directions ask for.
