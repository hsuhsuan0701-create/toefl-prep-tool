# Scored Speaking illustrations (severity anchors)

These are **this project's own illustrations, not ETS's samples**: an invented scenario, invented
interview questions, invented response transcripts, and scores this project assigns against its own
rubrics (`rubric-listen-and-repeat.md`, `rubric-interview.md`). They exist to show where the band
lines fall, especially the lines this task most often gets wrong. ETS publishes its own scored
samples in the *Updated TOEFL iBT® Test Overview* (2026), pp. 17–20; a user who installs those as
`references/official/samples-scored.md` (see `docs/official-rubrics.md`) gets them in place of this
file.

Transcripts are written the way this app's Ears pass produces them: fillers kept, pauses marked
with an ellipsis or a bracketed length, self-repairs shown with a dash.

## What ETS's own samples establish

Every scored Speaking response ETS publishes earns a **5**: seven Listen and Repeat responses and
four interview answers. There is no published Speaking sample below 5, so everything said here about
bands 0–4 is reasoned from the rubric descriptions, not from an ETS exemplar.

The interview samples are the important ones. Across four short answers they contain roughly
twenty-five filled pauses (*um*, *uh*), silences in the middle of clauses, self-repairs, a word
repeated while the speaker searches, *like* used as a filler, rising intonation on a statement, and
several uncorrected agreement and number errors. All four still earn a 5, and ETS's comment treats
the pauses and fillers as a normal part of speech that leaves the meaning intact.

The rule that follows, and that the illustrations below apply:

> **Disfluency is not a deduction.** A transcript makes natural speech look broken: every *um* and
> pause a listener never noticed is printed. Ask the rubric's question instead — did the pausing
> make the delivery choppy enough to interfere with following the answer? Countable pauses are not a
> choppy pace. Likewise, deduct for grammar only when an error blurs what was meant.

## Listen and Repeat — the recycling center

**Scenario.** You are starting a shift as a volunteer at the campus recycling center. Listen to the
coordinator and repeat what she says. You will hear each sentence once.

| # | Sentence | Words |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Please wear gloves at all times. | 6 |
| 2 | Paper and cardboard go in the blue bins. | 8 |
| 3 | Check each bottle for food before you rinse it. | 9 |
| 4 | Glass is collected on Tuesdays, but metal goes out on Fridays. | 11 |
| 5 | If a label is hard to remove, you can leave it on. | 12 |
| 6 | The scale by the loading door records what we collect each day. | 12 |
| 7 | Volunteers who finish their training early can help sort the electronics. | 11 |

### Responses to sentence 7, by band

Target: *Volunteers who finish their training early can help sort the electronics.*

| Response transcript | Score | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Volunteers who finish their training early can help sort the electronics. | 5 | Exact, every word clear. |
| Volunteers who finish the training early can help sort the electronics. | 4 | One small grammatical word changed (*their* → *the*); the meaning is unchanged. |
| Volunteers who finished their training early can help sort the electronics. | 4 | A tense ending changed; the meaning survives. |
| Volunteers who fin— who finish their training early can help to sort electronics. | 4 | A self-repair the speaker completes, one word added and one dropped. Still the same instruction. |
| Volunteers who finish training can help sort electronics early. | 3 | A full sentence with most content words, but *early* now modifies the sorting, so it no longer says what the coordinator said. |
| Volunteers who finish their training can help with the electric. | 3 | Complete, but a content word is lost and another changed in substance (*electronics* → *electric*); the meaning has shifted. |
| Volunteers who finish their… um… sort the electronics. | 2 | The opening and the last words come back with the middle gone; not a sentence on its own, and the meaning is in fragments. |
| Volunteers… electronics. | 1 | A few words, most of the sentence missing. |
| Sorry, I didn't catch it. | 0 | No attempt at the sentence. |

**The two boundaries that matter.** 5/4 is strict: any departure at all drops below 5. 4/3 turns on
whether the meaning survived, not on how many words moved — the self-repaired response with two
small changes is a 4, while the response that moved one word (*early*) is a 3. Dropping a single
small grammatical word never pushes a response to 3 by itself.

## Take an Interview — a study of student commuting

**Scenario.** A university research team is studying how students travel to campus, and you have
volunteered for a short online interview. The researcher has four questions for you.

1. (factual) First, how do you usually get to campus, and how long does the trip take you?
2. (experience) Tell me about a time your trip to campus went badly. What happened, and how did you
   deal with it?
3. (opinion) Some students think living close to campus is worth paying higher rent. Others would
   rather save money and accept a long commute. Which choice makes more sense to you, and why?
4. (opinion) Many cities are considering free public transport for students. Do you think that is a
   good use of public money? Why or why not?

### Responses to question 4, by band

**I-1 — Score 5**

> Um, yeah, I think it's a good use, actually. Because, uh… a lot of students, they works part-time
> and they don't have a car, so the bus is… is the only way. And if it's free, um, they can take
> the job that is a bit further away, or — or live in a cheaper area. Also, like, it's good for the
> city too? Less cars, less traffic around the campus. So, um… yeah, I think the money comes back
> in a way, because students can work more and spend in the city.

**Why 5.** On the question and well developed: a position, two connected reasons, and a closing
point that ties them together (**topical elaboration**). The transcript is full of fillers, a
repeated word, a self-repair and rising intonation on a statement, and has errors (*they works*,
*less cars*) — none of which blurs the meaning. Heard aloud, an answer like this moves at a normal
conversational pace with natural pauses, which is the band-5 line for **pace & pausing**. This is
the answer the rule above protects.

**I-2 — Score 4**

> I think it's a good idea. Students don't have much money. The bus is expensive for them. Free
> transport helps them go to work. It also helps the traffic. [pause, 3 s] So I agree with it.

**Why 4.** On the question and developed, but the ideas sit side by side with no links between them
— the band-4 line for **topical elaboration**. The long pause before the conclusion slightly
interrupts the flow (**pace & pausing** at 4). The language is accurate but conveys general
meanings rather than precise ones (**grammar & vocabulary** at 4). Compare I-1: messier on the
page, better as an answer.

**I-3 — Score 3**

> Free bus… [pause, 4 s] I think, um, is good. Because… [pause, 3 s] students… uh… no money. And,
> um… [pause, 5 s] the city… is, uh… is more good.

**Why 3.** Generally on the question, but the development is thin: one reason, half stated. Unlike
I-1, the pauses here are long and frequent enough that the delivery is choppy and hard to follow,
which is the band-3 line for **pace & pausing** — weighed separately from the thin elaboration. The
limited grammar (*is more good*, *students… no money*) noticeably restricts what the speaker can
say.

**I-4 — Score 2**

> Free public transport for students… public money… um… good use of public money. Yes. Students.

**Why 2.** The answer barely connects to what was asked, and most of its words are borrowed from the
question, with no development of its own — the band-2 line for **topical
elaboration**. Very little grammar or vocabulary is on show.

**I-5 — Score 1**

> Bus… um… money… [pause, 6 s] yes.

**Why 1.** Isolated words, loosely related to the question.

## Reading the transcript, and its limits

- The anchors above are written as transcripts, and the transcripts this app scores are produced by
  speech recognition. Recognition tends to drop fillers, tidy up grammar, and substitute a likely
  word for an unclear one. So a transcript can look word-perfect when the spoken response was not,
  and a strange word in it may be the recogniser's guess rather than the speaker's error.
- For pace, pausing, pronunciation and intonation, rely on the Delivery Report and on what the
  Judges heard in the audio. File a pronunciation finding under the `pronunciation-proxy`
  category, which tells the learner it was inferred rather than observed directly.
